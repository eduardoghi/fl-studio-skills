# Music Composition

Help the user compose original music. Treat music theory as a tool for making musical decisions, not as proof that a composition sounds good.

## Developing musical ideas

A repeated passage is not necessarily musical development.

When developing an existing idea:

1. Identify the primary musical idea. It may be melodic, rhythmic, harmonic or textural.
2. Determine what makes that idea recognizable.
3. Consider how it could evolve while preserving its identity.
4. Experiment with one intentional change, such as rhythmic variation, phrasing, rests, melodic contour, harmonic interaction or register.
5. Compare the original and modified versions before adding more instruments.

Adding instruments, increasing volume, doubling octaves or applying effects can improve an arrangement, but these actions alone do not demonstrate compositional development.

Repetition is not inherently bad. It may be essential to the style. Do not introduce arbitrary variations simply to avoid repetition.

## Verification

When comparing two musical ideas:

- Preserve the original.
- Change only the musical element being tested.
- Keep instrumentation, tempo, effects and accompaniment consistent.
- Export both versions for listening.
- Explain the actual musical change without claiming that it sounds better.

Objective audio measurements cannot determine whether an idea is memorable, expressive or emotionally effective.

The listener makes the artistic decision.

If neither version works, reconsider the underlying idea rather than automatically adding layers.

## Composing interacting musical parts

A composition is not merely a chord progression with independent
instrument patterns layered on top.

When creating a multi-instrument passage, consider how its musical
parts interact over time.

### Identify musical functions

For each significant part, identify its current function:

- Foreground: carries the listener's attention.
- Harmonic support: establishes or colors the harmony.
- Rhythmic movement: creates pulse, momentum or rhythmic tension.
- Counterpart: responds to or contrasts with another part.
- Texture: contributes atmosphere, space or evolving timbre.

These are descriptive functions, not mandatory instrument roles.
A single instrument may change functions during the passage.

### Compose relationships

Consider interactions such as:

- One instrument answers a phrase from another.
- A sustained line creates space for rhythmic movement elsewhere.
- The bass changes the harmonic meaning of sustained upper notes.
- A rhythmic figure changes when the main phrase becomes active.
- A melodic fragment moves between instruments.
- A previously active part becomes silent to create contrast.
- A supporting figure becomes prominent later in the composition.

Do not apply all these techniques automatically.

A new part should have a reason to exist beyond increasing the
number of simultaneous instruments.

### Develop sections musically

When developing a section, identify:

1. What musical idea carries over from the previous section?
2. What changes in the behavior or relationship of the parts?
3. What expectation does the new section establish or resolve?

Do not equate development with adding instruments, raising the
melody by an octave, increasing velocity or making the mix louder.

Those techniques can support development, but do not guarantee it.

Repetition and static textures can also be intentional and
musically effective.

### Compose in context

Do not assume every composition needs a prominent lead melody.

An idea can emerge from rhythm, texture, harmony, instrumental
interaction or a combination of these.

Avoid composing instruments as unrelated MIDI patterns and
expecting their simultaneous playback to create cohesion.

Develop the main musical relationships together, while keeping
the arrangement simple enough to evaluate.

### Review

Before rendering, inspect whether each part has a distinct
purpose and whether those purposes evolve during the passage.

This inspection checks the intended arrangement, not its
artistic success.

After rendering, report what was actually implemented.
Do not claim that the parts interact beautifully or that
the composition is emotionally effective without perceptual
evidence.

The listener makes the final artistic decision.
